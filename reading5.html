Reflection #5: The Risking Risk, and How We Stop It

When reading The Risking Risk, and How We Stop It, I was entranced with the article especially when it came to the interactable web-page design and animations as you go deeper into the article. I particularly liked the animations following every scroll as I read, especially with the transitions of the fire zooming in on every scene that each sentence describes. With how the unique web-page interactions, animations, photos, and data visualization are displayed; it enhances the text in a sense that shows the urgency of the situation and the looming threat W.J. Hennigan is trying to make. The threat being the increasing risk of a nuclear attack especially with how strained and tense the relationships between the major countries (that hold nuclear weapons) are.

The web page interacts with the reader by following their scrolling with an animation. In the beginning, you are already brought to an animation (slow flashing red background fading in and out) that resembles much of an alarm when it comes to incoming catastrophic attacks. As you scroll, you are brought to the first section of the article which is “Opinion: The Brink,” and then flashes white before transitioning to the article itself. I think this is an amazing example of a web-page interaction that can hook the viewer in. Especially with the animations, they truly visualize the looming threat of a nuclear attack. If it was left in simple text, the reader would not grasp the severity of the threat compared to a visual of a possible disaster.

Adding with the photos and videos, for example with President Vladimir Putin's speech and Defense Secretary Lloyd Austin, it shows that this is not a fantasy or something that the public can simply brush off. Hennigan makes it clear with the animations and interactable web-page that this is a severe threat that the public should take seriously. With the photos and videos of these credible and well-known politicians speaking of these topics regarding war and nuclear weapons, this shows that this is our reality. Our reality being the fact that a nuclear bomb can be dropped any second now and cause a catastrophic disaster and cost the lives of numerous people.

With the data visualization, this not only shows the severity of the threat as well, but a much clearer picture on the threat that we soon can be dealing with if these tensions continue. In the visualization of a data regarding Nuclear Arms over the years (from 1945), there shows a massive spike in nuclear weapons around 1987 (especially from Russia) but eventually shrunk back down in 2023. Although the nuclear weapons seemed to have lessened based on the data, the threat is still very much there. The public should not sway their opinion from the threat even if it has statistically shrunk down, and we should still acknowledge and be aware of the threat regardless if the statistics say otherwise. Hennigan describes this beautifully in the article, how “In the decades since, overall nuclear stockpiles have shrunk, but the number of nuclear powers has increased to nine.”
