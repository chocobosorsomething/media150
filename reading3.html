Reflection #3: Motherhood in Crisis, My Stolen Future

For Motherhood in Crisis, I chose Chapter 2: The Schoolgirl (Heela) for My Stolen Future. What I noticed about the illustrations varying from each page was how the colors begin to dim as she describes even further on what happened to her to have been pregnant years before. Especially when she was still rather young. There was one particular panel of Heela and her boyfriend (Patrick), where he held her tightly and forced her to have sex with him, that there was a slight red shading on their figures that represented much of the tension and danger that Patrick was about to inflict on her. Especially with how Heela seemed to cower as Patrick held her, the colors (and the blank background as well) show the suffocating atmosphere Patrick is trying to create to intimidate her and Heela's helplessness with his aggression.

Progressing forward into the story as well, there are two particular panels where you can see Heela and the counsellor of Rainbo Centre (Neneh) together. What makes these two visually interesting is the fact that Neneh wasn't drawn the way Heela was; Neneh was a photograph, and Heela (alongside the pen Neneh was holding and writing on the paper) was drawn in that cartoony style. I think, with this abrupt change in style, this is a clever way to show that support is truly and physically there for girls who need it and are going through the same situation as Heela was. If Neneh was drawn in the same cartoony style as Heela was, the message regarding support for these girls wouldn't be as powerful, especially since there may be people who would doubt or question the validity of the experiences girls go through. Also, with the way Neneh dresses, the blue and yellow complementing each other gives this sense of empowerment and comfort she has to help girls struggling similarly to Heela's experience. Especially with how Heela says at the end of this panel, “...I felt really safe.” Neneh's outfit shows her presence as someone to hold onto and see as comfort for girls like Heela.

The illustrations in this chapter discussing Heela's experiences definitely heighten the message the chapter was going for compared to if it were to be text-only. Each illustration shows the suffering Heela had to endure under sexual abuse, and these illustrations especially handle sensitive topics like teen pregnancy and mentions of abortion rather nicely because you (as a reader) are able to sympathize and understand where they are coming from. For example, when Heela suggested aborting the unborn baby to her friend, her friend tells her not to go along with it because many girls in their village die when they get an abortion, and that most of it happens in secret. If this were text-only, the reader would not be able to digest sensitive content like this as well as seeing it vividly. I think photography would also be a much stronger element, but to me the illustrations make more sense for Heela's anonymity to share her story if that was what they were going for.
