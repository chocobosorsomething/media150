Reflection #2: Your Brain on ChatGPT & Brain Experts WARNING: Watch This Before Using ChatGPT Again!

After reading the study Your Brain on ChatGPT, it made me reflect on how LLMs have been slowly integrating themselves into our society and how misusing them can lead to a reduction in human cognition and knowledge. Throughout the study, one of the three groups (LLM group) was recorded to have shown weaker “neutral connectivity and under-engagement of alpha and beta networks,” whereas the Brain-to-LLM group showed the complete opposite, where they demonstrated higher memory recall and “re-engagement of widespread occipito-parietal and prefrontal nodes” (MIT Media Lab). While this study doesn't show that LLMs are essentially making humans dumber and less intelligent (which is also shown in their QnA where the researcher denies such claims), it does seem to be limiting our processes to making our brains work such as thinking when it comes to writing essays. These LLMs, though very valuable most of the time, can also be greatly misused when people only see these tools as a mere convenience when dealing with assignments most people would consider tedious to deal with themselves. This would lead to engagement with the assignment lowering drastically, especially as shown in the study when the LLM group was forced to use no tools (LLM-to-Brain) compared to the Brain-only group participants (Brain-to-LLM).

This study regarding LLMs and how they reduce human cognition is also discussed in this video by The Diary Of A CEO titled Brain Experts WARNING: Watch This Before Using ChatGPT Again! In it, two renowned scholars and doctors of their respective fields converse about the study, much similar to the one in Your Brain on ChatGPT. When watching the video, it gave me more insight into this study because I was able to see two experts be able to debate about it visually, even when I'm not sitting at the table with them physically. One particularly interesting point that Dr. Daniel Amen brings up regarding the people who do not remember what they wrote minutes later, even with the help of LLMs, is that they are not part of the experience of writing it. So there's no way that the information gets encoded in your brain unless you are interacting with it. I find this point of his particularly interesting because of the reason he brought up, which is the experience of writing the paper. If you completely depend on someone else, even an LLM, to write it for you, it's not considered your work because you did not put the proper brainpower into it, other than writing the prompt to make the LLM write it for you. If you interact with the material in the slightest bit, it's much more efficient than having to merely copy and paste the LLM's response or just read it over. I think the experience of writing an essay is also the most important for the information to stick in your brain in the long run, especially since you are working and teaching your brain more knowledge regardless of how tedious the task may be.
